Best chicken coop lighting for egg production

Hours of light for laying hens, the morning the bulb should come on, and how many bulbs cover the coop and the run. Not a heat-lamp page.

Built by Jeremy Panasuk. Published November 10, 2026.

Quick answer: Penn State Extension says laying hens need 14 to 16 hours of light a day, added in the morning so birds can roost at sunset, and never more than 16 hours. University of Arizona Extension suggests one 9-watt LED or 60-watt incandescent bulb per 200 sq ft of coop and run. A 10-hour winter day needs 4 to 6 extra hours.

The 4 to 6 extra hours are derived: 14 − 10 and 16 − 10. Penn State publishes 14 to 16 hours. The 10-hour day is the example in that sentence. Arizona's December–January range is 9 to 10 hours, depending on latitude.

What 14 to 16 hours means on a short day

Penn State Extension says layers require 14 to 16 hours of light daily. Add artificial light in the morning, and allow the birds to roost at sunset. One foot-candle is full light for birds. Use a 25- to 40-watt bulb. Do not light adult birds for more than 16 hours daily.

University of Arizona Extension says hens should receive 14 to 16 hours of light daily. Put the added light in the hours before sunrise so hens roost with the natural sunset. If some or all of the extra light is after sunset, dim it over 30 to 45 minutes, because hens have poor night vision. Do not leave lights on 24 hours. A photoperiod greater than 16 hours does not stimulate additional egg production. Stop the lights when natural daylight matches the schedule, and start again when natural daylight falls below 14 hours.

Arizona says daylight there runs from 9 to 10 hours in December and January up to 14 to 15 hours in May through July, depending on northern or southern Arizona. A 9-hour day needs 5 to 7 extra hours to reach 14 to 16. That gap is derived: 14 − 9 and 16 − 9. A 10-hour day needs 4 to 6 extra hours, derived the same way from 14 and 16.

The morning clock time is sunset minus the target, which is how Arizona's 14-hour table is built. For Tucson, week of January 1, the table lists sunrise 7:26 AM, sunset 5:29 PM, 10 hours 3 minutes of daylight, and lights on at 3:30 AM. Sunset minus 14 hours is 3:29 AM. The table prints 3:30 AM. This calculator prints the exact subtraction and does not invent a different morning. A 16-hour morning is the same subtraction from Penn State's upper end. Arizona's table does not publish a 16-hour column.

Arizona's article says lights need to come on as early as 3:20 AM in the darkest weeks. The Flagstaff table on that same page lists 3:05 AM for the weeks of December 2 and December 16. Those are both on the page. This calculator does not lock either clock time in. It uses the sunrise and sunset you enter.

Bulbs: two extension sentences, one count

Arizona, for a small backyard flock: one 60-watt incandescent bulb, or a 9-watt LED of the same brightness, per 200 square feet of coop and run. Spread the light so the floor is not full of shadows. Keep the bulb clean. Nesting areas should stay dim; a curtain over the nest boxes is the method that page names. Choose a warm, red-orange wavelength rather than a cool one. Hang the bulb so birds and moisture cannot disturb it.

Penn State's 25- to 40-watt bulb is a different sentence. It is not a per-square-foot rate, and it is not the 9-watt or 60-watt bulb Arizona names. A third Penn State housing page, already quoted on the flock-size lighting section, says one low-intensity bulb (40 watts or less) every 40 feet, and that a bird needs only 1 foot-candle. The count on this page uses only Arizona's one bulb per 200 sq ft of coop plus run. It does not average the wattages.

Worked example: Tucson, week of January 1

Inputs are Arizona's published Tucson row, not a guess. Sunrise 7:26 AM, sunset 5:29 PM. Daylight is 10 hours 3 minutes. To reach 14 hours, the gap is 3 hours 57 minutes (derived: 14 hours minus 10 hours 3 minutes). Lights on at 3:29 AM, which is sunset minus 14 hours. Arizona prints 3:30 AM for that week. To reach 16 hours, the gap is 5 hours 57 minutes (derived: 16 hours minus 10 hours 3 minutes) and the light would come on at 1:29 AM. Penn State says not to go past 16 hours, so that second morning is the top of the range, not a start toward an all-night bulb.

On bulb count, 200 sq ft of coop plus run is one 9-watt LED or one 60-watt incandescent, the Arizona rate with nothing left over. The form starts at 200 sq ft of coop and no run so the sum is that published unit. A remainder above 200 still counts as another bulb. Pullets, timers, and a missed morning

Arizona says pullets should be on a naturally declining light schedule from 8 to 20 weeks, because early laying can lead to prolapse and smaller eggs. If the older flock is on a long-day schedule, delay the fall lights until the pullets are laying age, then restart when they are 18 weeks old. Add only 15 to 30 minutes a week until you reach 14 hours. Any change for adult hens is the same size step: 15 to 30 minutes a week. Adjust the clock every couple of weeks as sunrise and sunset move. An automatic timer is what that page tells you to use.

Arizona says forgetting the lights for even one or two days can drop egg production for a while, or start a molt that drops production for 6 to 8 weeks.

This is not a heat lamp

Arizona says a heat-producing bulb is unnecessary when the coop protects the birds, that heat lamps carry a large fire risk and should be avoided, and that you should not use a heat lamp as the light that is there to support laying. A heating panel is the alternative that page names if the coop actually needs heat. Adult heat is a separate argument, on the winter heat-lamp guide. The building, drafts, and floor space are on the cold-weather coop design page.

Common mistake

Adding the extra hours at night and switching them off at once. Arizona wants the added light before sunrise so the birds go to roost with the sun. An evening addition has to dim over 30 to 45 minutes. The other mistake is leaving the bulb on past 16 hours. Penn State says not to light adult birds more than 16 hours daily, and Arizona says more than 16 hours does not produce more eggs.
