Best chicken coop design for cold weather

The winter building: floor space with a run and without one, then the vents. Heat is a different page.

Built by Jeremy Panasuk. Published November 10, 2026.

Quick answer: Oregon State University Extension says a coop does not need insulation as long as it has no drafts, and passive vents in walls, gables, eaves or the roof should clear ammonia and moisture. Plan 3 sq ft per bird when hens can use a run, and 8 to 10 sq ft per bird when they can't get outside.

Winter floor checker

Oregon State University Extension, EC 1644: provide at least 3 square feet per bird if there is access to a run or outdoor area, and 8 to 10 square feet per bird if there is no outdoor access. The checker multiplies those rates by the flock and compares the product with length times width. Both products are labeled derived. It does not invent a third rate.

Can the hens get outside?

The 4 by 6 foot example is 24 square feet. For 6 birds that is 4 sq ft per bird, derived as 24 ÷ 6. Oregon State does not specify a 4 by 6 floor. The other flock-size rates on this site are on coop size by flock.

A 4 by 6 foot floor is 24 sq ft (derived: 4 × 6). Six birds at that floor have 4 sq ft each (derived: 24 ÷ 6). That meets the "at least 3" line and misses the 8 to 10 line. Six birds with no outdoor access need 48 to 60 sq ft (derived: 6 × 8 and 6 × 10). Run size, as its own number, is on how big a chicken run should be. Oregon State's 3 sq ft figure assumes the run exists. It does not replace the run-size page.

Vent checklist

Tick the items the two extension pages actually name. Neither page publishes a fan size or a cubic-feet-per-minute rate. Oregon State says passive ventilation is adequate for most backyard coops, and to build in as much ventilation as possible without drafts or a way in for predators.

The boxes are a list to walk with. They are not a score.

Insulation, stated twice and not averaged

Oregon State says coops do not need to be insulated, but be sure there are no drafts. Ventilation removes ammonia fumes, carbon dioxide, and moisture, and brings in fresh air. Passive air moves through windows in walls or vents in gables, eaves, or roofs.

University of New Hampshire Extension says some people insulate, which helps the inside stay warmer, but insulation is not an absolute necessity because keeping wind off the birds matters more. A cold wind takes away the birds' ability to keep themselves warm. Seal gaps. The roof should be solid and leak-proof. Siding other than wood, including corrugated metal, works if it keeps moisture and wind out. Vent holes or a small burlap-covered window, away from the prevailing wind, let moisture and ammonia from droppings escape. That opening should not be big enough for a predator.

What New Hampshire says about heat

Read on November 10, 2026, the UNH winter-care page does not tell you to hang a heat lamp. Under "To Heat or Not to Heat" it says: in most circumstances, advise against adding supplemental heat, because the fire risk is too high with combustible bedding and because chickens knock things over. A coop that is properly constructed and mostly draft-free, except for ventilation, is what that page says should limit the danger from the cold if you have followed the rest of the article. On the coldest days it suggests petroleum jelly on combs and wattles (the page spells them "waddles") to help prevent frostbite. Petroleum jelly is not described as a treatment for frostbite that is already there. If you suspect frostbite, UNH says to seek a veterinarian.

The exception UNH names is smaller breeds. If you raise bantams, you may consider insulating the coop and/or carefully using a specially designed heater for chicken coops, and you should read the warnings before you install it. That is a heater made for coops, not a lamp hung over the roost.

Oregon State, in the maintenance list, says to install electric lights and that they will also serve as a heat source during extremely cold weather. That sentence is not the same as UNH's advice against supplemental heat. This page does not pick a wattage and does not blend the two. The pages that disagree about adult heat, and the brooder schedule, are on the winter heat-lamp guide.

UNH's light note belongs with hours of light, not with a heater: when daylight drops below around 14 hours, supplemental light can bring the day back to 14 to 16 hours, using a lamp that will not generate too much heat. LED bulbs are the ones that page says do not make the heat of an incandescent or a fluorescent. The morning schedule and the bulb count are on the chicken coop lighting for egg production page.

Roosts, nests, and the shell

Keep the roost inches apart. They are not one number.

  • Oregon State: in general, 1- to 2-inch square wood with the top edges rounded, 2 feet off the floor, 10 inches between poles, and 5 to 10 inches of pole per bird. Silkie poles should be only 1 foot off the ground. Wood that is pressure-treated or coated in latex-based paint is the easy-to-clean option that page names. Poles that are too small or too round may cause foot problems.
  • New Hampshire Extension: about 9 to 12 inches of roost per chicken, wood rather than metal, about 1 to 2 inches wide so the toes can wrap the edge. Metal can hold the cold and lead to frostbitten feet.

The year-round roost pages on this site use other extension figures. They are compared on how much roost space per chicken. Do not average Oregon State's 5 to 10 inches with New Hampshire's 9 to 12.

Oregon State says one nest box for about every four birds, about 12 inches deep, wide, and tall, not on the ground and not lower than the roosts. A roof sloped at 45 degrees keeps birds off the top of the boxes. For the shell: close the flock in at night, raise the coop at least 1 foot off the ground, use a concrete slab or solid wood floor, enclose the run in 1- by 2-inch mesh with netting or wire over the top, and bury the fence 6 inches to 1 foot. Doors should open outward. Those are design lines from EC 1644. They are not floor-area rates.

Common mistake

Sealing the vents because the coop feels cold. Oregon State wants no drafts and still wants the passive openings. New Hampshire wants the wind gaps closed and still wants a vent on the lee side. The other mistake is building the 3 sq ft floor and then locking the birds in with no run. Three square feet is the rate for birds that can get outside. Birds that cannot get outside are the 8 to 10 sq ft rate.
