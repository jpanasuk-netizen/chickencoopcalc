How many nesting boxes per chicken?

Published guidance is one nest per 4 to 5 hens. Box size, height, and bedding still decide whether the hens use them.

Quick answer: One nesting box per 4 to 5 hens: six hens need 2 boxes, twelve need 3. Hens share boxes, so extra boxes buy little. This site's calculator counts one per 3, which is tighter than that range.

The rule and the chart

1 nesting box per 4 to 5 hens. UC Master Gardeners, Maryland Extension, and Utah State Extension all state that rate. The chart below uses one per 4, the tighter end. Six hens need 2 boxes; twelve need 3.

The standard column is one box per 4 hens, the tighter end of the published 4-to-5 range. The second column adds a box for broody-prone breeds (Silkies, Cochins, Orpingtons), which is a site allowance, not a second published ratio. Penn State's count is different: two nests for the first four hens, then one more per four additional hens. What Maine, Maryland, and Georgia say about a hen that is already sitting — and what the nest function returns when she is moved off the shared boxes — is on how to care for a broody hen.

Box specs that matter

  • Dimensions: Penn State says a chicken nest should be at least 12×12 inches. Utah says 12 to 15 inches high and wide, and at least 12 inches deep. Maryland's table uses a 10×10 inch box. A larger box is not a published requirement for heavy breeds on these pages.
  • Height: 18–24 inches off the floor, and always below the roost bars — boxes above roosts become sleeping spots, and sleeping hens poop where they sleep. No roost bars over the boxes, ever.
  • Lip: 4 inches minimum on the front to hold bedding and eggs.
  • Bedding: Penn State says to keep about two inches of clean, dry nesting material (pine shavings preferred). Maryland says one to two inches of straw or pine shavings. Bare boxes crack eggs.
  • Access: external collection doors save the daily walk-in — the single best quality-of-life feature in coop design.

Why more boxes doesn't mean more eggs

New keepers over-buy boxes expecting each hen to want her own. Instead, hens share — and often queue for one specific favorite while identical boxes sit empty. One per 4 to 5 still works because the favorite turns over and the rest are overflow. If queueing breaks eggs, a dummy egg in an empty box spreads the traffic, or you add one box.

Nesting boxes vs coop size

Boxes come out of your coop floor budget, not in addition to it. A 24 sq ft six-hen coop (4×6 ft) fits two 12-inch boxes on one wall with barely a footprint — about 2 sq ft total — but badly placed boxes, ones hens sleep in or refuse, push laying into corners and eat your effective floor space. Get the coop sizing right first, then hang the boxes low, dark, and below the roosts.

Building boxes vs buying them

A roll-out floor sends the egg to a collection tray, so the hen does not reach it and the egg stays out of the bedding. These pages do not publish a price for a plywood box or a plastic box. They also do not publish a 14 by 14 inch nest. Penn State's minimum, already in the FAQ, is at least 12 by 12 inches.

Placement still decides whether hens use the box: the darkest corner of the coop, away from the pop door's draft, with the front lip at roost-step height so hens hop up rather than jump. If a box sits unused, it is usually too bright, too low, or catching wind. Move it before blaming the hens.

FAQ

How many nesting boxes for 6 hens?

Two boxes at one per 4 to 5 hens. Penn State says at least 12×12 inches, with the nest at least 2 feet off the ground. Utah says no less than 18 inches off the floor. Keep the boxes below the roost.

Do hens need individual nesting boxes?

No. The published rate is one shared box per 4 to 5 hens, not one box per hen.

What do I put in the nesting box?

About two inches of clean, dry pine shavings or straw, which is what Penn State and Maryland describe. Replace material that gets wet or coated with a broken egg. These pages do not give a cedar warning.
