How many nesting boxes per chicken?

One box per 3–4 hens. That's the whole rule — but box size, height, and bedding decide whether the hens actually use them.

The rule and the chart

1 nesting box per 3–4 hens. A hen lays an egg roughly every 25 hours — not daily on the clock — so a box serves three to four layers without ever being fully occupied. Six hens need 2 boxes; twelve need 3–4.

Broody-prone breeds (Silkies, Cochins, Orpingtons) occupy a box for weeks at a stretch, which is when you size to the 3-hen end of the rule.

Box specs that matter

  • Dimensions: 12×12×12 inches for standard breeds; 14×14 for Brahmas and Jersey Giants. Smaller feels safer to hens; bigger invites two birds and broken eggs.
  • Height: 18–24 inches off the floor, and always below the roost bars — boxes above roosts become sleeping spots, and sleeping hens poop where they sleep. No roost bars over the boxes, ever.
  • Lip: 4 inches minimum on the front to hold bedding and eggs.
  • Bedding: 3–4 inches of pine shavings or straw, topped up weekly. Broken eggs in bare boxes teach egg-eating, and that habit is nearly untrainable once learned.
  • Access: external collection doors save the daily walk-in — the single best quality-of-life feature in coop design.

Why more boxes doesn't mean more eggs

New keepers over-buy boxes expecting each hen to want her own. Instead, hens share — and often queue for one specific favorite while three identical boxes sit empty. The 1-per-3 rule survives this because the favorite turns over in minutes and the rest are overflow. If queueing ever breaks eggs, fake eggs or golf balls in the empty boxes spread the traffic, or you add one box. Buying double never fixes it — it just adds square footage the flock ignores.

Nesting boxes vs coop size

Boxes come out of your coop floor budget, not in addition to it. A 24 sq ft six-hen coop (4×6 ft) fits two 12-inch boxes on one wall with barely a footprint — about 2 sq ft total — but badly placed boxes, ones hens sleep in or refuse, push laying into corners and eat your effective floor space. Get the coop sizing right first, then hang the boxes low, dark, and below the roosts.

Building boxes vs buying them

DY boxes are a half-sheet of plywood and an hour: cut five 14×14 pieces (back, bottom, two sides, front lip), screw them into a wall stud, done — a 2-box unit costs $30–40 in material versus $60–150 for plastic roll-out boxes. What the commercial boxes buy you is the roll-out floor: eggs roll gently back to a collection tray the moment they're laid, which ends egg-eating (hens can't reach eggs they never touch) and keeps eggs clean out of the bedding. For six hens the DIY route is the better value; for a 12+ hen flock where collection time compounds daily, the roll-out units pay for themselves in saved minutes and unbroken eggs.

Placement details that decide whether hens approve: the darkest corner of the coop, away from the pop door's draft, with the front lip at roost-step height so hens hop up rather than jump. If a box sits unused for more than a week, it's usually too bright, too low, or catching wind — move it before blaming the hens. And put a golf ball in each box at setup: it shows pullets what the box is for and shortens the find-the-nest phase by weeks.

FAQ

How many nesting boxes for 6 hens?

Two 12×12×12 boxes, mounted 18–24 inches up, below the roost bars, with 3–4 inches of bedding. If the flock is Silkies or Cochins, plan on two still — the broody-buffer matters at this flock size only if one goes broody in spring.

Do hens need individual nesting boxes?

No — shared boxes are the norm and the 1-per-3 standard exists precisely because hens lay on a ~25-hour cycle and queue politely. Individual boxes per hen is a myth that doubles your build cost and halves the space hens actually use.

What do I put in the nesting box?

3–4 inches of pine shavings or straw, refreshed weekly and topped after every broken egg. Avoid cedar (aromatic oils irritate bird lungs) and never use moldy straw — damp boxes breed the exact conditions that cause egg-eating and broodiness.
